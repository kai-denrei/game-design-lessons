Aphorisms of the craft

Lessons of the Game Makers

Six designers, six theories of fun — and the lines that run between them.

I

Twenty Lessons

Two decades steering Magic: The Gathering distilled into twenty imperatives. Rosewater's throughline is blunt: design for the human you have, not the one you wish you had.

  1. Fighting human nature is a losing battle. Change the game to fit people, not people to fit the game.
  2. Aesthetics matter. Players expect balance, symmetry, and pattern completion — perception is a spec.
  3. Resonance. Borrow the emotional associations the audience already arrives carrying.
  4. Piggyback. Front-load mechanics onto knowledge players already hold.
  5. Don't confuse interesting with fun. Intellect makes it interesting; emotion makes it fun.
  6. Know the emotion you're evoking. Every component serves that emotion or gets cut.
  7. Let players make it personal. Choice is what turns a game into their game.
  8. The details are where they fall in love. Players bond to the small, specific thing.
  9. Give a sense of ownership. Deep customization makes the game an extension of the player.
  10. Leave room to explore. Let them discover; don't lay everything bare.
  11. Liked-but-not-loved fails. Chase passion, not the absence of complaint.
  12. Don't design to prove you can. Ego is not a design goal.
  13. Make the fun path the winning path. Players optimise toward victory — put the fun there.
  14. Sometimes force the behaviour. Occasionally you must compel an action so players learn it's good.
  15. Design each component for its audience. Pleasing everyone pleases no one.
  16. Fear boredom more than challenge. Players forgive ambition; they resent tedium.
  17. You needn't change much to change everything. Tweak a foundation, not the surface.
  18. Restrictions breed creativity. A constrained start blocks the old neural path.
  19. Players spot problems, not solutions. Trust the diagnosis; doubt the prescribed cure.
  20. All the lessons connect. Design is one holistic view, not twenty separate ones.

Mark Rosewater · Head Designer, Magic: The Gathering · GDC 2016

II

Interesting Decisions

Meier's whole theory fits in a sentence — and most of its force is in what it rules out. A game is a series of interesting decisions; the craft is keeping each one genuinely interesting.

  • The core. A game is a series of interesting decisions.
  • Defined negatively. A decision is not interesting if everyone always picks the same option — or if the choice is effectively random.
  • What makes one interesting. Meaningful, visible consequences; real trade-offs; long-term against short-term; choices that express a playstyle.
  • Be the player's partner. Feedback must let them see that their choices mattered.
  • On testers. Almost always right about what feels wrong; almost always wrong about how to fix it.

Sid Meier · Civilization · aphorism GDC 1989, expanded GDC 2012

III

The 400 Project

An attempt to write down the field's rules of thumb — four hundred of them, eventually. It reached about a hundred and twelve. A selection, with the authors' own caveat attached: these are guidelines, not laws.

  • Fight player fatigue.
  • Maximize expressive potential.
  • Maintain a consistent level of abstraction.
  • Concretize ideas.
  • Provide clear short-term goals.
  • Identify constraints.
  • Begin at the middle.
  • Make the game fun for the player — not the designer or the computer.
  • Make the effects of the AI visible to the player.
  • Create the AI in the player's mind through suggestion.
  • Don't take away hard-won possessions from the player.

They later cautioned that these are rules worth knowing partly so you know when to break them.

Noah Falstein & Hal Barwood · The 400 Project · GDC 2001 onward — reached ~112 of a planned 400

IV

A Theory of Fun

Koster reframes fun as cognition. It is the brain's pleasure in learning a pattern — which carries an unsettling corollary about where every game is headed.

  • Fun is learning. The act of mastering a problem mentally — the brain's pleasure in grasping a pattern.
  • The corollary. A game's destiny is to become boring once its pattern is learned. Fun is the process; routine is the destination.
  • Good games teach. A game stops being fun when there's nothing left to learn.
  • Dressing is not mechanic. Theme, art, and story are not the system — separate the two when you design.
  • A grammar of gameplay. Mechanics combine like an atomic grammar; read it and you can critique as well as build.

Raph Koster · A Theory of Fun · Austin GDC keynote 2003, book 2005

V

Water Finds a Crack

Johnson's laws are about the adversary you didn't expect: your own player, optimising. Give them the chance and they will optimise the fun right out of the game.

  • The first law. Given the opportunity, players will optimise the fun out of a game.
  • The corollary. A designer's job is partly to protect players from themselves.
  • Water finds a crack. Players treat a game as an optimisation puzzle — most reward, least risk, lowest variance — and seep through any gap you leave.
  • Complexity discipline. If you add something new, take something old out.

Soren Johnson · Lead Designer, Civilization IV · column “Water Finds a Crack,” 2011

VI

The Deck of Lenses

Not rules but questions. Schell offers roughly a hundred and sixteen lenses — each a set of questions you turn on your own design. The method is the message: see the work from as many angles as you can. A handful, as illustration.

The Lens of Essential Experience
What experience do I want the player to have — and is the game actually delivering it?
The Lens of Surprise
What will surprise the player? Where have I stopped surprising myself?
The Lens of Fun
What parts are fun? What parts should be more fun?
The Lens of the Player
What do they expect, what do they want, and what do they not yet realise they want?
The Lens of Flow
Is the challenge tracking the player's growing skill — neither boring nor overwhelming?

Treat these as a sampling. The full deck — every lens, spanning story, mechanics, technology, aesthetics, psychology, and business — is the actual artifact.

Jesse Schell · The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses · 2008; deck, 116 cards

VII

Synthesis

Six lists, one craft. Read together, the great designers' aphorisms stop looking like six theories and start looking like one argument told from six angles.

They converge, first, on what fun is. Against the temptation to chase cleverness, they agree that fun is emotion and learning, not intellectual interest alone — Rosewater's fifth lesson and Koster's whole thesis are the same claim in different registers.

They share, second, an account of the player. The player is an optimiser: they chase victory and will flatten fun to get it. So the designer aligns the fun path with the winning path and, where that fails, protects players from themselves — Rosewater's thirteenth lesson, Meier's interesting decision, and Johnson's water finding its crack are one continuous thought.

They agree, third, on the enemy. The designer's ego is the adversary, and the audience is the client — Rosewater's twelfth lesson and the 400 Project's “fun for the player, not the designer” say it plainly.

Where they split is method. Meier and Koster offer a single deep principle; Falstein, Barwood, and Rosewater offer enumerated catalogues; Schell offers an interrogative deck. The same craft seen through three epistemologies — one law, a catalogue of rules, a set of questions.

Which returns us to Rosewater's twentieth: the lessons are not twenty, or a hundred and twelve, or one. They are a single interconnected view of how play is made.

A reading across all six sources.
